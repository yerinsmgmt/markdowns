# Cover image prompts

23 images. Twenty courses and resources, then three programmes.

Generate them, then name the files by number and drop the folder anywhere.
`1.png` is prompt 1, `2.png` is prompt 2, and so on to `23.png`. Any of
`.png`, `.jpg` or `.webp` is fine. I map them to the right products from the
number, so the numbering is the only thing that has to be right.

---

## Read this before generating any of them

**Paste this block at the start of every prompt.** It is what makes 23
separate generations look like one catalogue instead of a folder of stock
photos. The per image text follows it.

> Wide 16:9 photographic image, 1600x900. **A Black person at work, doing the
> thing the course teaches.** One or two people, never a crowd. Natural
> daylight, soft shadows, shallow depth of field. The person is absorbed in
> the work, mid task, with visible competence and calm focus: this is somebody
> the viewer would want to be. Real environments, warm neutrals and off white,
> with one restrained accent of deep blue. No text, no letters, no numbers, no
> logos, no watermarks, no readable screen content. Nothing glowing, no
> circuit boards, no robots, no blue neon, no floating holograms. Leave clear
> space in roughly a third of the frame for a title to sit over.

### Why each of those rules is there

**A person, and a Black person.** This is the rule that changed, and it changed
because the first set of images was wrong. The old block said "no people
looking at the camera" and asked for "real objects and real workspaces", and
what came back was twenty pictures of empty desks and laptops. They are not
badly made. They are unrelatable: a bare screen says a tool exists, it does not
say what a person becomes by learning this, and nobody looks at a desk and
thinks "I want that to be me".

The academy sells a role, not software. `sell-the-outcome` in the product notes
already says every name and description must say what the learner can build or
become; the imagery was contradicting the copy. A person mid work, visibly good
at it, is what makes somebody want the role.

Black people specifically, because the audience is African first and a
catalogue full of white professionals tells that audience the product was made
for somebody else.

Two things this does not license. Nobody grinning at the camera holding a
laptop, which is the stock photography failure mode and reads as an
advertisement rather than as work. And no fake diversity crowd shots: one or
two people, doing something real.

**No text.** Generated lettering is almost always subtly wrong, and it is the
first thing that makes a page look cheap. Every title on the site is real text
rendered above the image, so the image never needs to carry words.

**Empty space in a third of the frame.** These get cropped to 16:10 in the
swipe rows on the dashboard and the course pages. A subject filling the whole
frame loses its head or its edges.

**Nothing glowing, no robots, no circuit boards.** The spec is blunt that the
academy sells the outcome and not the technology. A picture of a glowing brain
sells "AI". A picture of a desk mid work sells "you will be able to do this".

**One accent of deep blue.** The brand colour, which is `--brand` `#0b4ab8`
since the phase 1 palette work in `17-ui-refresh-direction.md`. It was deep blue
when these prompts were written. One accent per image is what makes
a row of them look deliberate. Every image being deep blue makes the catalogue look
like a single tinted wall.

**Locked cards show these at reduced opacity.** So the composition has to read
when it is faded. Strong simple shapes survive that. Fine detail does not.

# Second batch: two promo photographs, and people

## Why there were no people in the first twenty

Fair question, and the answer is that I wrote the rule that caused it. The
shared style block said "no people looking at the camera" and every prompt was
built around objects and empty workspaces.

I did that for two reasons and one of them was wrong. The right reason: AI
faces are unreliable at small sizes and a bad one is worse than none, and
still life keeps twenty separate generations looking like one catalogue. The
wrong one: I optimised for the catalogue holding together and forgot what a
catalogue is for. A desk with a notebook tells somebody what the subject is. A
person mid work tells them what it would be like to be that person, and that
is the thing that makes somebody click.

So: people belong in the adverts, where the whole job is to make somebody feel
something, and hands belong in the course covers about doing a thing. Pure
still life is right for the reference material, the prompt packs and toolkits,
where the subject really is a document.

These two are the adverts, and they matter most.

`teams.jpg` and `certificate.jpg`. Not numbered, because there are only two
